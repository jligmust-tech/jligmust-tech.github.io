Using 500 Agents in Parallel

What has changed in agent orchestration, where massive parallelism helps, how to design a reliable system, and why “500 agents” is a capacity decision — not automatically an intelligence decision.

A companion review, Hallucination in the Swarm, covers the failure side of the same arithmetic: correlated errors, false consensus, contagion, and an eight-family test battery for measuring collective hallucination.

Scope note: this is an editorial research brief based on established literature, engineering practice, and selected practitioner case studies. No code, benchmark, or agent workload was executed for this report. Literature findings, reported case-study observations, proposed designs, and illustrative calculations are labeled separately; estimates are not measured results.

Executive summary

Running 500 agents in parallel means distributing many bounded reasoning or tool-using jobs across a scheduler, model providers, and an isolated execution environment. It is most valuable when a problem can be decomposed into relatively independent investigations: searching a large document collection, generating many candidate designs, classifying records, testing variations, or asking independent reviewers to critique the same artifact.

Parallelism changes the shape of an AI application. Instead of asking one long-running agent to do everything serially, a coordinator creates a work plan, fans out tasks, collects evidence, and reduces the results into a decision. The result can be faster coverage and better resilience to an individual agent’s mistake. The cost is coordination: duplicated work, inconsistent outputs, rate limits, security exposure, difficult observability, and a potentially large increase in token and tool spending.

Central thesis: 500 workers are useful only when the workload has enough independent work and a trustworthy aggregation method. For tightly coupled tasks, 500 agents may create 500 opportunities for disagreement while adding little useful parallelism.

What does “500 agents” mean?

The phrase can describe several different systems. A useful report should name the unit being counted:

InterpretationWhat it countsImportant qualification
500 logical tasksFive hundred prompts or jobs in a queueConcurrency may be throttled to 20, 50, or another safe limit.
500 model callsIndividual inference requestsOne “agent” may make several calls, so agent count understates demand.
500 active sessionsStateful agents with memory and toolsRequires isolation, lifecycle management, and much more observability.
500 independent opinionsRepeated analyses of one questionCorrelated model errors mean this is not the same as 500 independent experts.
500 workers in a workflowParallel stages plus retries and reducersUsually the most practical interpretation: a pool with controlled concurrency.

Concurrency is the number running at one moment; throughput is completed work per unit of time. A system can have 500 queued jobs and only 50 in flight. This distinction is essential for estimating capacity, spend, and provider limits.

Why parallel agents are becoming practical

1. Better orchestration primitives

Modern workflow systems provide queues, DAGs, durable state, retries, cancellation, timeouts, backpressure, and fan-out/fan-in operations. These primitives turn a collection of prompts into a recoverable distributed workflow rather than a fragile script.

2. More capable structured output

Schema-constrained responses, tool calling, typed events, and validation make it possible to pass machine-readable findings between workers. A reducer can compare evidence fields rather than attempting to interpret 500 unconstrained essays.

3. Model and provider diversity

Applications can route easy subtasks to small, inexpensive models and reserve stronger models for synthesis, ambiguity, or quality review. Multiple providers and regions can improve availability, although routing also complicates evaluation, privacy, and cost accounting.

4. Elastic compute and isolated tools

Containers, serverless jobs, browser sandboxes, vector databases, and distributed data-processing frameworks make it easier to provision short-lived workers. Tool isolation is especially important when agents can execute code, browse, modify files, or call business APIs.

5. Retrieval and larger context windows

Workers can retrieve only the documents relevant to their assignment, reducing the need to place an entire corpus in every prompt. Larger contexts help synthesis, but they do not remove the need for relevance filtering, provenance, and context-budget discipline.

Important nuance: advances reduce orchestration friction; they do not make reasoning independent, factual, or free. More agents can amplify a bad instruction, a poisoned source, or a flawed decomposition.

Recommended control points

  • Admission control: cap active work by provider, tenant, tool, region, and risk class.
  • Task contracts: specify input, output schema, allowed tools, evidence requirement, deadline, and stop condition.
  • Leases and idempotency: make retries safe and ensure a timed-out worker does not continue mutating state.
  • Evidence store: retain source identifiers, excerpts, timestamps, worker/model versions, and confidence — not only final text.
  • Reducer: deduplicate claims, detect contradictions, weight evidence, and send uncertain cases to review.
  • Kill switch: cancel a run, revoke credentials, and stop tool access without waiting for every worker to finish.

Additional idea 1: keep agents synchronized without making them wait on everything

Design thesis: synchronize the assignment, evidence versions, and commit rules — not every thought or tool call. The goal is a consistent research process, not 500 copies of the same conversation. The following architecture is a proposal, not a tested 500-agent deployment.

What the literature and engineering reports support

Documented mechanism: LangGraph’s Pregel runtime uses bulk-synchronous steps: plan the active actors, execute them in parallel, then publish channel updates. Writes made during execution become visible in the following step. This offers a concrete reference for round-based coordination; it is not evidence that 500 LLM workers will achieve a particular speedup. [S1: LangGraph runtime documentation]

Reported limitation: Anthropic’s research-system account identifies heavily shared context and inter-agent dependencies as poor fits, and notes difficulty with real-time agent coordination. The design inference is to isolate work between explicit exchange points rather than build one giant group chat. [S2: Anthropic, June 13, 2025]

Three synchronization choices

PolicyProposed useTrade-off

Asynchronous evidence streamWorkers independently publish versioned findings; reducers build provisional views.Early results are available sooner, but late evidence can invalidate a provisional synthesis. Final release still needs a coverage gate.

Proposed synchronization contract

  1. Freeze the brief per round: pin run ID, round ID, objective version, source snapshot, schema, definitions, allowed tools, and deadline. A snapshot is an explicit source manifest; it does not freeze the live internet.
  2. Assign ownership: give every partition a task ID and authoritative assignment record. Use the scheduler’s lease generation or fencing token to reject late writes from an expired assignment.
  3. Keep evidence append-only: workers submit their own artifacts; they do not overwrite a shared final draft. Each artifact identifies its task, attempt, snapshot, evidence, and superseded result if any.
  4. Separate control from findings: the coordinator may announce a changed brief or cancellation. Workers must not interpret instructions found inside a source document as control messages.
  5. Check freshness at commit: accept only artifacts matching the active assignment and declared snapshot. A newer source can be retained as a proposed update, not silently substituted into the old round.
  6. Define completion explicitly: track required partitions as accepted, missing, failed, cancelled, or superseded. Release a partial report only when permitted, with gaps visible; never equate a response-count quorum with complete coverage.

Illustrative failure: worker 117 loses its lease while reading a document; a replacement finishes first. The original worker later returns a different result. The runtime rejects its stale lease generation as authoritative, while retaining the artifact for audit. A prompt alone cannot enforce this.

Estimated communication comparison

Illustrative arithmetic, not a network benchmark: if every worker sends one directed update to every other worker in a round, 500 × 499 = 249,500 deliveries result. One submission per worker to a coordinator plus one acknowledgment back is 1,000 deliveries. In the 20-group proposal, 500 worker submissions + 20 group summaries = 520 upward submissions, excluding acknowledgments, broadcasts, retries, and source transfers. These are different communication contracts — not equal-work throughput measurements.

Do not oversynchronize: a barrier after every action serializes progress around the slowest participant. Proposed policy: use barriers only at dependency boundaries, preserve independent first-pass research, and exchange compact evidence deltas rather than full transcripts. Measure barrier wait, stale-artifact rejection, coverage, and contradiction survival.

Additional idea 2: short-context and instant workers may be the better default

Hypothesis, not a universal ranking: narrow tasks may benefit more from small evidence packets and low-latency workers than from a powerful model reading the entire shared history. Treat this as a routing question to evaluate, not a claim that smaller models are always smarter.

Distinguish the four knobs

  • Context capacity: the maximum supported input window. A large-capacity model can still receive a short prompt.
  • Actual context length: the evidence and instructions supplied for one task. Compact inputs do not require choosing a small-capacity model.
  • Model capability and size: separate from context capacity; a small model can support a large window.
  • Inference effort and latency: “instant” here means a low-latency or low-deliberation configuration, not a standardized model category or a guarantee of immediate completion.

Proposed model-routing design

Use a capable planner to create bounded tasks. Give each worker the objective, shared definitions, its assignment, a few relevant source excerpts, and a compact output contract. Start low-risk extraction, tagging, and citation-location tasks on a fast configuration; route cross-document reasoning, disputed evidence, and final synthesis to stronger models. Retrieve more context before escalation when missing evidence — not model capability — is the bottleneck.

Escalation triggers: missing mandatory evidence, incompatible definitions, contradictory sources, unsupported conclusions, or an output-contract failure. Do not use the worker’s self-reported confidence as the sole routing signal. Review a sample of apparently successful fast-worker outputs as well, because silent errors never ask for help.

Proposed evaluation — not an experiment performed here

Use the same held-out assignments and source snapshot in a 2 × 2 comparison: compact versus full context, and fast versus stronger model. Add the compact-plus-escalation policy as a fifth arm. Hold concurrency, tool access, scoring rubric, and completion criteria constant; report quality at equal budget as well as cost at equal accepted quality. Include difficult multi-document cases and tasks whose evidence sits across chunk boundaries. Measure citation correctness, required-fact recall, accepted results per unit cost, escalation rate, and p95 end-to-end latency.

Blog angle: a large fleet may need smaller working memories, not a larger shared chat. Keep workers focused, keep evidence recoverable, and spend stronger-model reasoning on ambiguity and synthesis.

Estimated comparison: serial versus parallel

The following scenario is deliberately illustrative: 500 independent tasks, each requiring one model request, with an average end-to-end worker duration of 30 seconds. It excludes queue delay, retries, synthesis, provider throttling, and tool execution.

Execution planNominal active workersIdeal worker completion timeOperational interpretation
Serial1500 × 30 s ≈ 4 h 10 minSimple but slow; one failure can delay the whole run.
Moderate pool2520 waves × 30 s ≈ 10 minOften easier to fit within rate limits and budgets.
Large pool1005 waves × 30 s ≈ 2.5 minGood latency if provider and tools sustain the burst.
Nominal 500-way fan-out500≈ 30 sTheoretical lower bound; real systems add startup, limits, retries, and reduction time.
Cost does not fall because wall-clock time falls. If every task uses the same input and output tokens, the model-token bill is approximately the sum of all 500 calls. Parallel execution improves elapsed time and throughput, not the underlying amount of work.

As a rough planning formula:

total cost ≈ Σ(input tokens × input price + output tokens × output price) + tool/compute/storage cost + retries

wall time ≈ queue delay + ceil(tasks / effective concurrency) × service time + reduction time

“Effective concurrency” is usually less than the configured number because of rate limits, uneven task lengths, cold starts, retries, and shared tools. A realistic capacity test should measure p50 and p95 latency, completion rate, token use, duplicate work, and quality — not only average duration.

Where the approach delivers value

Lower latency

Independent work can complete in waves rather than waiting for one long serial chain.

Broader coverage

Different prompts, sources, search paths, and review lenses expose cases a single trajectory may miss.

Fault containment

A failed or confused worker can be retried or discarded without losing the entire run.

Specialization

Workers can receive narrow roles, tools, models, and policies suited to their subtasks.

Elastic throughput

Queues can absorb demand and scale capacity for bursts, subject to provider and budget controls.

Auditable evidence

A well-designed run preserves many small decisions and sources instead of one opaque answer.

Shortcomings, failure modes, and risks

Coordination overhead

Planning, serialization, retries, deduplication, and synthesis can consume enough time and tokens to erase the benefit of parallelism. The more dependencies a workflow has, the less it behaves like a parallel workload.

Correlated mistakes

Five hundred calls to the same model, with the same prompt and sources, are not five hundred independent experts. They may share hallucinations, blind spots, prompt sensitivity, and source bias. Diversity must be designed and measured, not assumed from worker count.

Quality dilution and weak aggregation

A reducer can overvalue majority opinion, count duplicated claims as agreement, or compress away important dissent. A fluent synthesis can hide the fact that its evidence is thin. Structured claims and citation-level verification are safer than voting over prose alone.

Cost and rate-limit pressure

Bursting 500 requests can hit quotas, exhaust credits, trigger provider throttling, or overload a shared database and browser pool. Retries during an outage can create a retry storm unless they use backoff, jitter, and a global budget.

Security and privacy expansion

Every worker and tool connection increases the attack surface. Prompt injection in one retrieved document can influence downstream work; broad credentials can turn a harmless analysis into a destructive action. Use least privilege, sandboxing, content isolation, secret redaction, and approval for irreversible operations.

State and consistency problems

Concurrent writes create races, duplicate actions, stale reads, and difficult rollback. Prefer immutable outputs and append-only evidence for analysis. For actions, use transactions, idempotency keys, locks where necessary, and compensating operations.

Observability burden

Five hundred traces are too many to inspect manually. Without correlation IDs, cost attribution, sampled logs, and aggregated failure taxonomies, operators cannot explain why a run failed or whether it improved.

Do not grant broad autonomous permissions merely to demonstrate scale. Start with read-only research or sandboxed simulations. Introduce real actions only behind narrow scopes, policy checks, human approval, and reversible workflows.

Practical adoption playbook

  1. Start with a decomposition test. Label task pairs as independent, loosely coupled, or tightly coupled. Parallelize only the first two categories initially.
  2. Define a quality contract. Choose measurable targets: factual accuracy, recall, citation completeness, pass rate, cost per completed task, and p95 latency.
  3. Benchmark small pools. Compare 1, 5, 25, 100, and 500 logical workers while holding the task set constant. Look for the point where quality plateaus or cost and errors accelerate.
  4. Use progressive concurrency. Ramp gradually, apply per-provider and per-tool limits, and stop when error rate, queue age, or spend crosses a threshold.
  5. Make outputs typed and traceable. Store claim, evidence, confidence, source, worker version, and timestamp as fields. Keep raw output for audit, but do not use it as the only data contract.
  6. Separate analysis from action. Let a large pool propose or verify; let a small, policy-controlled stage approve changes to external systems.
  7. Evaluate the reducer. Test whether synthesis preserves minority evidence, correctly resolves conflicts, and refuses to make claims unsupported by workers.
  8. Plan graceful degradation. If the provider, tool, or queue is unavailable, return partial results with explicit coverage and uncertainty rather than fabricating completeness.

Parallel-run readiness checklist

This is a copyable operator checklist for confirming synchronization, model routing, actual concurrency, failure handling, and release readiness. It is intentionally separate from the prompts: prompts describe worker behavior, while the runtime must enforce leases, quotas, permissions, and cancellation.

Acceptance rule: never describe a run as “500 agents running simultaneously” unless runtime timestamps and capacity telemetry establish overlapping execution. A queue containing 500 logical tasks is not that evidence.

Status: proposed operational checklist, not an execution record. No code, benchmark, or agent workload has been run. Unchecked items mean unverified, not failed.

1. Define what “500” means

  • Record whether 500 means logical assignments, active sessions, concurrent requests, or reserved worker slots.
  • Record the total required source partitions separately from review and synthesis tasks.
  • Set an approved concurrency ceiling; do not assume the provider supports 500 simultaneous calls.
  • Count reviewers, retries, and reducers against capacity when they share the same resources.
  • Define whether the goal is faster completion, broader coverage, better quality, or lower cost per accepted result.

2. Freeze the shared contract

  • Approve a versioned objective, definitions, exclusions, and evidence requirements.
  • Pin a source manifest and snapshot identifier; retain source dates and locations.
  • Set a run ID, round IDs, schema version, task IDs, and partition IDs.
  • Specify what changes require a new round and which existing artifacts remain compatible.
  • Define required versus optional coverage and the conditions for a partial release.
  • Assemble 00-shared-contract.md plus the appropriate role from the prompt pack for each assignment.
  • Remove unresolved placeholders before dispatch.

3. Establish real concurrency outside the prompts

  • Use a scheduler that can launch independent ready tasks concurrently.
  • Enforce per-provider request and token limits and per-tool capacity limits.
  • Prevent a task from starting before its required dependency artifacts are accepted.
  • Record dispatch, start, finish, and acceptance timestamps from the runtime.
  • Distinguish time waiting for a model, active model service, and tool execution where telemetry permits.
  • Use start/finish intervals to establish overlap; do not infer simultaneous execution from the number of submitted prompts.
  • Report peak and time-weighted average in-flight requests separately from active sessions.

Illustrative reporting language: “500 logical source-review tasks ran with a configured ceiling of 100 active tasks.” Only add an observed concurrency figure when runtime records support it. Do not say “500 ran simultaneously” merely because the queue contained 500 tasks.

4. Choose synchronization boundaries

  • Choose strict barriers, asynchronous provisional results, or grouped milestones for each dependency stage.
  • Keep independent first-pass findings private until their initial submission when independent judgment matters.
  • Share the brief, schema, and control messages — not an ever-growing global transcript.
  • Require compatible snapshot and brief versions before aggregation.
  • Preserve append-only artifacts and identify which results supersede earlier attempts.
  • Verify current lease generation at commit; reject expired owners’ authoritative writes.
  • Define barrier timeout and reassignment rules without silently dropping missing partitions.
  • Retain unresolved contradictions across local and global reduction.

5. Route compact and fast workers safely

  • Keep context capacity, actual packet length, model capability, and inference effort separate in the manifest.
  • Ensure compact packets contain the definitions and evidence required for the task.
  • Provide a way to request missing context without guessing.
  • Route low-risk extraction and tagging to fast workers only after an appropriate quality assessment.
  • Escalate conflicting evidence, ambiguous definitions, and difficult cross-document reasoning.
  • Independently audit a sample of apparently successful fast-worker outputs, not only failures.
  • Account for retrieval, expanded packets, reviewer work, outputs, and escalation in total cost.
  • Verify current model availability, prices, and provider limits before making product-specific claims.

6. Bound failure and permission risks

  • Enforce read-only permissions for this survey workload outside the model.
  • Keep secrets out of prompts and isolate untrusted source text from control instructions.
  • Configure timeouts, bounded retries, backoff, and jitter.
  • Deduplicate commits and prevent duplicate external actions where applicable.
  • Set hard spending limits and cancellation controls.
  • Ensure cancellation reaches tool sessions and pending work, not just the coordinator.
  • Do not let agents accept their own evidence or certify their own lease validity.

8. Release gate

  • Every required partition has an explicit terminal or unresolved state.
  • Final claims map to accepted evidence, not merely worker agreement.
  • Citation checks and reviewer decisions are retained.
  • Missing coverage, failed tasks, and unresolved contradictions are visible.
  • Estimates are labeled and not mixed with observed runtime metrics.
  • No benchmark results are claimed without actual measurements.
  • A named release authority approves the final artifact under the agreed completeness policy.

Runtime prerequisites — not implemented by these prompts

  • A durable task registry and dependency graph with uniquely identified partitions.
  • Actual concurrent dispatch, bounded by approved request/token quotas and per-tool limits; 500 logical tasks does not require 500 simultaneous calls.
  • Atomic assignment leases, expiry, monotonically increasing fencing generations, and commit-time verification.
  • Idempotent result commits, bounded retries with backoff/jitter, timeouts, and cancellation propagation.
  • Tool permissions enforced outside the model; source access separated from control messages.
  • An artifact store retaining source versions, provenance, raw results, and review decisions.
  • Token/cost accounting, coverage tracking, and an explicit final release gate.

- Split tasks so a compact packet retains the necessary definitions and evidence. Identify questions that cannot safely be split.
- Choose synchronization policy per dependency boundary: round barrier, asynchronous provisional results, or local-group milestone. Explain the choice.
- Include retrieval/context expansion, independent quality checks, and final synthesis in the total resource budget.
- Do not spend all 500 slots on workers if reviewers and reducers need the same active-slot budget. Distinguish task count from simultaneous-slot count.
- Identify shared bottlenecks and tasks requiring stronger reasoning.

Return the manifest, dependency summary, coverage map, proposed concurrency caps, quality rubric, release criteria, and explicit unknowns. If limits are not provided, mark them unresolved rather than inventing provider capacity or a monetary budget.
02-dispatch-coordinator.md 
Apply the shared contract. Given {{TASK_REGISTRY}}, {{ACCEPTED_ARTIFACT_INDEX}}, and {{RUNTIME_CAPACITY_AND_BUDGET}}, propose the next dispatch batch. Do not claim to launch jobs or acquire leases.

Select only tasks whose declared dependencies are accepted. Request atomic leases from the runtime rather than fabricating ownership. Respect the minimum of active-slot, request, token, tool, and budget constraints provided by the runtime.

For each proposed task, return task_id, model route, packet reference, dependency artifact IDs, proposed timeout, and dispatch reason. For each deferred task, state the blocking dependency or capacity constraint.

Prefer fast workers for low-risk extraction with adequate evidence. Route ambiguity, conflicts, and multi-source reasoning to escalation. No provider-specific speed or price assumptions without supplied verified data.

For retries, identify the failed attempt and use the runtime's retry policy; do not independently create unbounded retries. Escalate repeated failures. Report budget exhaustion, cancellation, and missing runtime data explicitly.
03-fast-worker.md 
Apply the shared contract. Complete this one narrow assignment: {{QUESTION}}. Evidence packet: {{PACKET_REFERENCE_OR_CONTENT}}.

Extract or classify only what the packet supports. Check units, dates, definitions, and source identity before concluding. Return concise atomic findings with exact source locations; do not produce a long narrative.

If a relevant fact is missing, list the precise missing document or passage and return needs_context. If evidence is present but requires complex reasoning, return needs_escalation with your partial supported findings. If sources disagree, preserve both; do not decide by majority vote.

Do not infer that omitted evidence does not exist. Do not claim the short packet represents the full corpus. Use the assignment's output limit, and prioritize evidence and missing coverage over generic explanation.
04-synchronization-review.md 
Apply the shared contract. Review {{ROUND_MANIFEST}}, {{ARTIFACT_METADATA}}, and {{TRUSTED_RUNTIME_VALIDATION_RESULTS}}. Produce a milestone recommendation; you are not the commit authority.

Check run, round, brief, snapshot, schema, task, attempt, and lease-generation compatibility against the authoritative registry. Treat runtime checks as required for lease validity and acceptance. If validation results are missing, recommend hold; do not assume validity.

Group artifacts into compatible, incompatible, duplicate, superseded, and pending-validation. Retain incompatible artifacts for audit, not authoritative synthesis.

For every required partition, report accepted, missing, failed, cancelled, or superseded using registry states. A worker saying completed is not an accepted partition. Distinguish optional reviews from required source coverage.

Recommend one of: continue waiting, bounded reassignment, start next dependency stage, release permitted partial output, or block release. List unresolved contradictions and the explicit release rule used. Do not treat a numerical response quorum as complete coverage.

If the brief changed, identify exactly which partitions require rework and which remain compatible. Never silently relabel old results with a new snapshot.
05-escalation-worker.md 
Apply the shared contract. Resolve {{ESCALATION_REQUEST}} using {{PRIOR_ARTIFACT}} and {{ADDITIONAL_APPROVED_EVIDENCE}}.

First classify the obstacle: missing context, ambiguous instructions, source conflict, inadequate reasoning, or unavailable tool. Request targeted additional context before more model effort when evidence is the actual problem.

Answer only the unresolved question, retaining prior supported findings. Link your artifact to the prior attempt; do not overwrite it. Distinguish corrected claims from merely expanded explanations.

For conflicting sources, compare date, scope, method, and directness. Explain why a conflict can or cannot be resolved. A stronger model is not an additional source.

Return resolved findings with citations, remaining uncertainty, coverage changes, and whether independent review is needed. If still blocked, stop and identify the missing input rather than inventing an answer.
06-evidence-reviewer.md 
Apply the shared contract. Independently review {{CLAIMS_TO_REVIEW}} against {{SOURCE_MANIFEST_AND_PASSAGES}}. Do not accept a claim because another worker sounded certain.

For each claim, mark supported, partly_supported, unsupported, contradicted, or unverifiable. Identify exact supporting and conflicting passages. Check numbers, denominators, dates, benchmark conditions, and whether a statement is a vendor report or an independent result.

Reject extrapolations that turn a small-scale result into a measured 500-agent outcome. Check whether citations actually establish the attached statement and whether multiple citations derive from the same origin.

Review the runtime-selected sample of apparent fast-worker successes as well as escalations. Do not select only easy successes yourself. Distinguish evaluation sample coverage from full verification.

Return claim decisions, corrected wording proposals, unresolved questions, and the source locations checked. Recommend acceptance; do not commit acceptance yourself.
07-group-reducer.md 
Apply the shared contract. Consolidate only {{ACCEPTED_GROUP_ARTIFACTS}} for {{GROUP_SCOPE}}.

Deduplicate equivalent claims by underlying evidence, not just wording. Preserve source and worker lineage. Keep disagreements and low-frequency but well-supported findings visible.

Produce a compact group summary, a claim-to-source index, an exceptions list, and a coverage ledger. Maintain references to full artifacts so compression does not erase evidence. Do not promote worker confidence into factual certainty.

Do not resolve incompatible snapshots by merging them. Request a synchronization review. Where a summary omits detail for length, retain a reference to the omitted material. Report missing partitions and reviewer decisions.

Stay within {{GROUP_OUTPUT_LIMIT}}. If the accepted input exceeds your capacity, request hierarchical batching without silently discarding artifacts.
08-final-synthesizer.md 
Apply the shared contract. Draft {{DELIVERABLE_FORMAT}} from {{ACCEPTED_GROUP_SUMMARIES}}, {{CLAIM_INDEX}}, {{EXCEPTIONS}}, and {{COVERAGE_LEDGER}}.

Use only accepted evidence for factual claims. Keep published results, proposed designs, and illustrative arithmetic visibly distinct. Cite the original source locations, not merely the reducer's conclusion.

Include benefits, synchronization overhead, failure modes, token economics, model-routing trade-offs, and unresolved questions. State the actual coverage denominator and any missing required partitions. Do not claim all 500 workers ran, finished simultaneously, or agreed unless runtime records establish that.

Preserve consequential dissent. Do not turn majority opinion into verification. If completeness requirements fail, recommend hold or label a partial draft only if authorized.

End with an editorial checklist: unsupported claims, unverifiable citations, stale evidence, omitted dissent, estimates that need labeling, and remaining release blockers. Do not invent benchmarks or execute tests.
09-evaluation-auditor.md 
Apply the shared contract. Design, but do not execute, an evaluation of {{PROPOSED_WORKFLOW}}. If measured records are supplied, audit them separately from the proposed study.

Specify five arms: compact context/fast model; full context/fast model; compact context/stronger model; full context/stronger model; compact-first with targeted context expansion and escalation. Treat context size and model capability as separate variables.

Use a held-out task set with a pinned source manifest, matched tools and concurrency, explicit quality rubric, and difficult cross-document cases. Avoid tuning routing thresholds on the final test set. Account for reviewer and reducer overhead.

Compare cost at matched accepted quality and quality at matched budget. Report citation correctness, required-fact recall, silent-error rate from audited successes, escalation rate, accepted results per cost, p50/p95 end-to-end latency, retries, coverage, and barrier wait. Request repeated runs and uncertainty reporting where practical.

For synchronization, compare strict barriers, asynchronous provisional updates, and grouped milestones on a matched dependency structure; do not pretend different communication semantics perform equal work.

List confounders: prompt caching, source chunking, provider limits, tool latency, output lengths, non-independent tasks, model versions, and verification effort.

If no runs were performed, label the entire output proposed evaluation and mark measured results unavailable. Never supply plausible-looking measurements.
Illustrative handoff — not an execution log 
Planner proposes 500 source-review partitions. Runtime dispatches only the approved ready subset, potentially in waves. Each worker receives the same versioned brief but only its own evidence packet. The runtime validates submissions and assigns review. Accepted artifacts feed local reducers, then a final synthesizer after the coverage gate. A separate escalation lane handles ambiguous tasks. Reviewers and reducers consume slots too if the fleet has a shared concurrency ceiling.

Before any real deployment:
- Replace placeholders and approve scope, permissions, budgets, and deadlines.
- Validate dependency and partition coverage before dispatch.
- Enforce ownership and cancellation outside the model.
- Preserve independent first-pass findings before exposing peers' answers.
- Use explicit synchronization milestones rather than all-to-all chat.
- Verify the final artifact against required coverage, evidence, and safety criteria.
- Do not claim this prompt pack guarantees concurrency, correctness, or measured performance.

Editorial takeaway

Editorial takeaway: the compelling story is not “500 agents are smarter than one.” It is “a carefully bounded pool can explore more of a decomposable problem in less elapsed time, while evidence, cost, and risk remain under control.”
