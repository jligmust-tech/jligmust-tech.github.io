Timeline review: from target to drug, 1982–2026

The toolkit post walked through six stages — target, structure, docking, affinity, generative design, selectivity — and the methods available at each. A timeline adds the one thing a method list cannot: which stages actually got better, and which merely got faster.

The answer is lopsided. Pose prediction improved by orders of magnitude. Affinity prediction improved modestly. Target validation and selectivity prediction barely improved at all, and those are the stages where molecules actually fail.

Scope note: companion review. The heatmap in §2 is an editorial maturity assessment on an ordinal 0–3 scale, not a measured performance score, and the cells name representative methods rather than all methods. No docking run, benchmark, or free-energy calculation was performed for this post.
Central claim: structure-based design automated the stages that have a geometric ground truth and left the stages that require a biological one. That is why a 2026 pipeline can generate a plausible binder for a wrong target in an afternoon, and why the field's failures are increasingly upstream of the part everyone benchmarks.

1. Why a timeline here

Drug discovery has an unusually clean external clock: the approval. Everything upstream is judged by a signal that arrives eight to fifteen years later, which means the field's methods are always evaluated on pipelines that started with a different toolset. Any claim that a new method improves success rates is therefore an extrapolation, and the historical record is the only place to check whether such extrapolations have ever held.

They have held exactly once, and not where people expected: structure prediction. AlphaFold did not improve clinical success rates; it removed a specific, expensive, well-defined bottleneck and made a whole class of downstream work cheap. That is a real contribution, and it is a different kind of contribution from "better drugs."

Figure 1. Maturity by stage, 1985–2026. "Infrastructure" means the stage is a service anyone can call, cheap enough to use by default. Two observations. First, the diagonal runs down and to the right: each stage matures later than the one above it, because each depends on the outputs of the one above. The last column is the one that matters for decisions — and it is the least blue.

3. The record

  • 1982–1999 · geometry and scoring · what improved: search
  • 1982DOCK — ligand–receptor interaction as a geometric matching problem; virtual screening becomes a computation.
  • 1990sAutoDock and empirical scoring — fast docking with fitted energy terms; the field acquires a tool and a habit of trusting scores that were never calibrated to affinity.
  • 1997–2000ChemScore, DrugScore — consensus and knowledge-based scoring; the number of scoring functions grows faster than the evidence that any of them ranks.
  • 2000–2013 · scale and free energy · what improved: throughput
  • 2000sStructure-based virtual screening at scale — million-compound libraries become routine; enrichment factors are reported, prospective hit rates are not.
  • 2005–13Cryo-EM revolution — structures of assemblies that crystallography could not reach; the structure bottleneck narrows from "any structure" to "the right conformation."
  • 2014–15FEP+ in production — relative binding free energy becomes accurate enough for prospective optimization on congeneric series, and expensive enough to stay expert-only.
  • 2017REINVENT and deep generative design — molecules generated by a model trained on chemistry; the objective function becomes the research problem.
  • 2018–2023 · learned structure and learned poses · what improved: the input
  • 2020–21AlphaFold 2 — high-accuracy structures at scale; docking pipelines that assumed a crystal structure are rewritten to assume a predicted one.
  • 2020Ultra-large virtual screening — billion-scale libraries and GPU-accelerated docking; the search space stops being the constraint.
  • 2023DiffDock — blind docking as generative pose prediction; the benchmark conversation shifts from enrichment to physical validity.
  • 2023RFdiffusion — de novo protein design by diffusion; binder design becomes a generation problem with a checkable objective.
  • 2024PoseBusters — a systematic check that predicted poses are physically plausible; a large share of learned docking outputs fail basic geometry, which reframes the field's headline metrics.
  • 2024–2026 · complexes and the validation gap · what improved: assemblies
  • 2024AlphaFold 3, Chai-1, Boltz-1 — co-folding of protein with ligand, nucleic acid, and ion; the unit of prediction becomes the assembly rather than the chain.
  • 2025Affinity from co-folding — models that emit a binding estimate alongside the pose; the honest reading is that pose is now good and affinity is still the open problem.
  • 2026This review — the pipeline's automation has moved upstream and downstream of the part that determines clinical success, which is target validation and selectivity.

4. The pipeline's weakest link is not the one with the papers

Two numbers frame the whole field, and neither is about docking. Attrition in clinical development is dominated by lack of efficacy and toxicity, not by bad poses. A model that improves pose accuracy by 20% therefore improves a quantity that is not in the causal path of most failures, while a model that improves target validation by 20% would be transformative and has no benchmark.

The timeline explains why this gap persists. Every stage in the heatmap that turned blue has a geometric or arithmetic ground truth: a structure, a pose, an energy, a synthesis route. Target validation has a biological ground truth that takes years and costs millions per data point, so the feedback loop is too slow to train on and too expensive to benchmark. Selectivity has the same problem in miniature: you can compute an off-target pose, but you cannot compute whether the off-target effect matters.

The structural problem, stated plainly: the field automated the stages where being wrong is detectable in an afternoon. It did not automate the stages where being wrong is detectable in a decade. That is a rational allocation of engineering effort and a poor allocation of scientific effort, and no amount of model scaling changes which stages have cheap checks.

5. What is actually benchmarked

The benchmark column is where the field's self-image is most misleading, because the standard tasks are retrospective and the interesting tasks are prospective.

StageStandard benchmarkWhat it can showWhat it cannot show
StructureCASP, blind, community-run since 1994Accuracy on unseen targets, comparablyWhether the conformation matters for the question
DockingPose reproduction and enrichment on decoy setsRanking within a curated libraryProspective hit rate; decoy bias is well documented
Pose validityPhysical plausibility checks (PP)Whether a pose is stereochemically possibleWhether it is the right pose
AffinityRetrospective correlation on congeneric seriesRelative ranking within a seriesAbsolute potency; scaffold hops; entropy and waters
Generative designComputational oracles, then synthesis and assayWhether the generator can satisfy a stated objectiveWhether the objective was the right one
SelectivityAssay panels, retrospectiveKnown off-targetsUnknown off-targets; the ones that cause failure

The one row with a genuinely blind, community-run, adversarial benchmark is structure — and it is also the row that became infrastructure. That correlation is not a coincidence. Blind benchmarks are what convert a method into a service, because they establish the conditions under which the method can be trusted, and services can only be trusted conditionally.

Docking has no CASP. It has decades of enrichment studies on decoy sets that everyone now knows are easier than reality, plus a growing literature on physically implausible poses. The consequence is visible in practice: groups that dock a billion compounds and then test a hundred hits report success rates that vary by more than an order of magnitude across targets, and nobody can say in advance which target will work. That is the signature of an uncalibrated method, not a bad one.

6. Where 2026 sits

  • Structure is solved at the chain level, useful at the assembly level, unreliable for dynamics. Co-folding made complexes routine; conformational change is still a research problem.
  • Pose prediction is good; pose validation is now the bottleneck. Plausibility checks are cheap and should be mandatory before any pose is reported.
  • Affinity is the field's central unresolved claim. It is the quantity medicinal chemists actually optimize, and it is the quantity the benchmarks measure least honestly.
  • Generative design is real but objective-limited. The generator solves the objective you wrote; most failures are the objective's fault.
  • Selectivity and ADMET remain data-limited. No architecture fixes a missing assay panel.
  • The next genuine improvement will be prospective. A pre-registered, multi-target, prospective hit-rate study would be worth more than another point of pose accuracy.

Closing thoughts

The honest summary of forty-four years is that structure-based design got dramatically better at answering the question it posed in 1982 — given a target and a structure, what binds? — and only marginally better at the questions that determine whether a drug exists: is this the right target, does the molecule do what the model says in a cell, and does it avoid the off-targets nobody has screened.

That is not a failure of the field. It is what happens when one part of a pipeline has a cheap, exact check and the rest do not. The part with the check accelerates until it is infrastructure, and the parts without one stay where they are, accumulating papers about how hard they are. The lesson for the next wave, including the agentic one, is to ask which stage of your pipeline has a checker, and to be suspicious of progress everywhere else.
