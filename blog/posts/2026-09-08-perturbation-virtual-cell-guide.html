Stage 1 guide to perturbation models, virtual cells, and biology-aware embeddings

This report turns the perturbation and virtual-cell literature into a practical starting map. The immediate target is not a new universal leaderboard. It is a defensible benchmark study: predict post-perturbation expression or cell-state distributions, test generalization to unseen perturbations and contexts, and determine whether a representation captures biology rather than an experimental shortcut.

Scope note: this is an information-only curation pass. It uses public links and summaries; no model, benchmark job, private database file or downloaded proprietary dataset is executed or included.

What belongs in Stage 1?

Stage 1 covers models that take a control state plus a perturbation and produce a predicted response. The response may be a point estimate of gene expression, a distribution of cells, or a structured embedding that is decoded by a lightweight predictor. This keeps the comparison scientifically coherent while allowing different model families to be tested on the same splits.

The accompanying Research Guide ranks the initial reading list by curation priority: direct task relevance, community visibility, reproducible code, and value for studying perturbation-aware representations. Its citation register records author, year, venue or preprint status, and a public paper/code path for each core entry. The rank is not a claim that one model is best across datasets.

Recommended reading order

  1. GEARS is the canonical genetic baseline. It combines a gene–gene knowledge graph with a perturbation model and explicitly targets unseen single and combinatorial perturbations. The Nature Biotechnology paper and official implementation are the right entry point. Its own repository also documents important limits: multi-cell-type transfer and combinatorial prediction require care about training coverage.
  2. CPA and chemCPA provide the compositional latent-variable view. CPA factorizes cell type, perturbation and covariates, while chemCPA adds chemical representations for unseen drugs. The CPA paper is useful for dose, cell-type, species and combination extrapolation; the chemCPA repository is the practical code anchor.
  3. scGen is the classic latent-shift baseline. Its VAE performs vector arithmetic between control and perturbed latent states, making it a useful test of whether a more elaborate model adds value beyond a simple response direction. See the Nature Methods paper and official code.
  4. scGPT and scFoundation are the main foundation-model probes. Treat them as representations plus a perturbation head, and make the head, tokenization, gene masking and data overlap explicit. The relevant primary sources are the scGPT paper and code and the scFoundation paper with its repository.
  5. CellOT and PerturbNet cover distributional prediction. CellOT uses neural optimal transport for unpaired treated/control populations; Perturbation response with neural optimal transport is the primary reference. PerturbNet is a deep generative model for unseen chemical and genetic perturbations; its paper and code should be evaluated with distributional metrics, not only mean expression.
  6. STATE, LPM and Scouter form the biology-aware extension. STATE separates a set-level cell-context embedding from a transition model and ships Cell-Eval. LPM is a large perturbation model designed around generalizable perturbation representations. Scouter uses GenePT text embeddings from gene descriptions, with reproducibility code at GitHub.

How to choose a model or agent tool

Choose by the endpoint and the kind of evidence you need, not by model size alone. For response prediction, use GEARS as the default genetic baseline, CPA/chemCPA for dose or context composition, CellOT/STATE/PerturbNet for distributions, and scGPT/scFoundation/LPM/Scouter when the central question is representation or biology-aware embeddings.

For agents, use scBench for concrete single-cell workflows, BixBench for long computational-biology trajectories, BioAgent Bench for robustness under failure modes, LAB-Bench for broad biology reasoning, and ClawBio when a local-first, MCP-compatible execution layer is the requirement. The full model selector and agent/tool selector list the public evidence, pros, cons and selection conditions for each entry.

Benchmark datasets: build a ladder, not a single split

The data layer should mix breadth with canonical, interpretable studies. scPerturb harmonizes 44 public perturbation-response datasets across RNA, protein and ATAC, while PerturBase expands the catalog to 122 datasets from 46 studies. These are indexes and harmonization resources, not permission to pool every study without an overlap and batch audit.

  • Norman: combinatorial two-gene perturbations in K562; use it to test whether a model composes unseen gene components.
  • Adamson: a canonical single-gene Perturb-seq screen; use it for held-out perturbations and systematic-variation checks.
  • Replogle: large K562 and RPE1 screens; use the processed access route and distinguish cell-line/context generalization from within-context prediction.
  • sci-Plex: multiplexed chemical responses across cell lines; use it for dose and chemical-context transfer.
  • Tahoe-100M: a modern large chemical atlas with more than 100 million profiles across 50 cancer cell lines. It is valuable for scale and context-aware models, but compute, access and pretraining overlap must be recorded explicitly.
  • OP3 / Open Problems: a community benchmark framing around 146 compounds in PBMCs, with public data through the Kaggle challenge.

Which benchmark suites should anchor the study?

scPerturBench is the broadest starting point in this map: 27 methods, 29 datasets, genetic and chemical perturbations, and separate context- and perturbation-generalization scenarios. Its Nature Methods paper is especially useful for the split and metric inventory.

Pair it with Systema and its code. Systema shows that systematic differences between perturbed and control cells can make ordinary metrics look better than the underlying perturbation-specific prediction. It therefore recommends perturbed-reference metrics and simple perturbed-mean or matching-mean baselines. A model should have to beat those baselines on the perturbation-specific signal, not merely reproduce an average shift.

PerturBench is a useful engineering harness for common data, splits and metrics, with a NeurIPS Datasets and Benchmarks paper. PertEval-scFM and its evaluation suite are valuable when the question is narrower: does a frozen foundation-model representation improve a lightweight perturbation predictor?

For set-valued predictions, use Cell-Eval. Its disjoint self-split estimates a data ceiling from the noise in the real cells, which keeps a low score from being misread as a model failure when the measured response itself is unstable.

Stage 1 embedding plan

The first embedding study should treat representation quality as a set of biological probes, not a single embedding score:

  • P0 — perturbation-aware cell embedding: retrieve the same perturbation across held-out contexts; separate control from response; classify response state with a frozen linear probe; and cluster cells by response state. Evaluate with perturbation holdouts and context holdouts.
  • P0 — perturbation embedding: test whether nearby vectors correspond to GO/Reactome-related interventions, known gene–gene interactions, or genetic-interaction classes. Compare GenePT, GO/STRING and shuffled-label controls so that a text prior is not treated as learned perturbation biology by default.
  • P1 — effect-size embedding: evaluate the perturbation-specific delta, not only the post-treatment state. Report top-DEG recovery, sign/direction accuracy, Pearson-Δ, centroid accuracy and RMSE-Δ.
  • P1 — context transfer: transfer a response to an unseen cell type, donor, species or disease state, with pretraining/test overlap disclosed. Do not use random cell splits as the only evidence.
  • P2 — mechanism and uncertainty: test pathway recovery, genetic-interaction prediction, confidence calibration and the gap to the Cell-Eval data ceiling. Interpretability and reconstruction should remain separate endpoints.

Suggested first information route

  1. Maintain a public-source ledger for Norman, Adamson, Replogle K562/RPE1, sci-Plex, Tahoe-100M and the broader scPerturb/PerturBase catalogs. Store landing-page links and citations, not local copies.
  2. Route genetic, chemical, distributional and embedding questions through the linked model families and benchmark suites. Keep source-reported claims separate from independently comparable results.
  3. Record the intended perturbation-exclusive, context-exclusive and both-exclusive split definitions as future evaluation specifications. For Norman, note the 0/2, 1/2 and 2/2 unseen gene-component cases.
  4. Record the planned control mean, perturbed mean, matching mean and linear/PCA baselines before the learned models. These are protocol guidance only; this site does not execute them.
  5. Link each P0 embedding probe to its biological readout, public prior and shuffled-control requirement. Audit possible leakage from perturbation tokens, gene identity and pretraining overlap.
  6. Use Systema, scPertEval and Cell-Eval as public reading/evaluation references; actual runs remain outside the current scope.

What stays on the roadmap?

The awesome single-cell foundation-model list is broad, and its perturbation/virtual-cell sections now include newer directions such as Tahoe-x1, GeneJEPA, X-Cell, SCALE and OCOO-T. These are important, but they should enter after the Stage 1 information router is stable; any future execution would be a separately authorized project. Tahoe-x1 scales perturbation-trained modeling, GeneJEPA targets predictive transcriptome embeddings, and X-Cell pursues diffusion-based genome-scale perturbation prediction. The awesome repo remains the living catalog for broader scFMs and virtual-cell proposals; the Research Guide keeps them visible as a backlog without implying that they have passed a common benchmark. A separate Stage 2 topic now covers bioinformatics-specific agents and AI tools from 2024–2026, starting with public references such as BixBench, scBench, BioAgent Bench and ClawBio.
