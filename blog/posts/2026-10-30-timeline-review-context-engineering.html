Timeline review: context engineering and model specialization, 2019–2026

The leaner-agents post argued that right-sizing models and shortening contexts improves results, and set that against the "don't build multi-agents" counterargument. A timeline shows that this is not a taste dispute. It is a sequence of measurements, each of which invalidated the previous era's default.

The sequence has three steps: the demonstration that long context is not uniformly better (2023), the demonstration that most queries do not need the largest model (2023–24), and the demonstration that more agents mean more tokens before they mean more accuracy (2025). Each step was empirical, cheap to run, and largely ignored until the cost bill arrived.

The curve in §2 is a schematic of the well-documented U-shaped position effect, drawn to the shape reported in the literature rather than digitized from a specific experiment. No model was evaluated for this post, and no cost figures here are measurements. Sections link back to the companion post; its argument is not restated here.
Central claim: context engineering became a discipline when two things were measured — that accuracy is not monotone in context length, and that cost is. Everything since (routing, cascades, compaction, sub-agents) is an engineering response to that pair of facts, and the response is better understood as budget allocation than as prompt craft.

1. Why a timeline here

This topic is unusually vulnerable to recency bias, because the defaults change every few months: windows grow, prices fall, and any claim about what is "too much context" is dated the moment it is made. A timeline separates three things that get conflated — what the models can accept, what they can use, and what you should pay for.

Those three curves move at different speeds, and most bad practice comes from treating the first as if it were the second.

2. The U-curve

short context (~8k tokens) long context (~200k tokens)

Figure 1. Not monotone. The shape is the finding: relevant information at the beginning or end of a prompt is used far more reliably than the same information in the middle, and the penalty grows with total length. The practical consequence is that a longer window does not buy proportional recall — it buys a larger space in which to misplace something. Every technique in §3 is a way of not relying on the middle.

3. The record

  • 2019–2022 · retrieval as a workaround · default: stuff the prompt
  • 2020Retrieval-augmented generation — retrieve then condition; the first architecture that treats context as a budget rather than a transcript.
  • 2021–22Vector search becomes infrastructure — embedding indexes make "the right four paragraphs" cheap to fetch, and make relevance ranking the new bottleneck.
  • 2023 · the middle problem, and the first measurements
  • 2023“Lost in the middle” — the U-shaped position effect documented across models and tasks; the strongest single result in the whole area, and the one most often cited without being applied.
  • 2023Cascades and routing — FrugalGPT shows that a cheap model answers most queries and a strong model handles the residue, at a fraction of the cost; the first formal statement that model choice is a per-query decision.
  • 2023Long-context marketing — windows grow by an order of magnitude; "needle in a haystack" retrieval is reported as the benchmark, which measures finding a token, not using it.
  • 2024 · routing, caching, and sub-agents
  • 2024Prompt caching — repeated prefixes become cheap, which quietly rewards stable system prompts and penalizes context churn.
  • 2024RouteLLM and learned routers — routing as a trained classifier with a cost-quality trade-off curve, rather than a hand-written if-statement.
  • 2024Chain-of-agents — long inputs split across workers that pass summaries to a manager; a structural fix for the middle problem instead of a prompting fix.
  • 2024Small models get good — sub-10B models become viable for extraction, classification, and formatting; the specialization argument stops being hypothetical.
  • 2025 · the bill arrives
  • 2025Orchestrator–worker at scale — production write-ups report large gains on breadth-first research tasks and large token multipliers; both numbers are reported in the same document, which is progress.
  • 2025“Context rot” — systematic degradation as context grows, even when retrieval is perfect; the effect is now a product concern rather than a paper finding.
  • 2025“Don't build multi-agents” — the counterargument that most workflows need one agent with a good context, not several with a shared one. The disagreement with orchestrator–worker is about task shape, not about capability.
  • 2026 · budget allocation
  • 2026Context budgets as an explicit design object — token budgets per agent, compaction policies, and eviction rules written down before the prompt is written.
  • 2026This review — the three curves (capacity, utilization, cost) are separated, and the practice is described as allocation rather than craft.

4. Cost first, accuracy second

The uncomfortable sequencing in this record is that the cost measurement usually came before the accuracy measurement, and it was the cost that changed behaviour. Long-context windows were adopted because they simplified engineering, not because they improved accuracy; routing was adopted because the invoice was visible; sub-agents were adopted because they parallelized latency, and only later was the token multiplier reported alongside the gain.

That is not a criticism of the engineering. It is a description of what actually drives adoption, and it has a practical implication: if you want better context engineering, put cost on the dashboard next to accuracy, because that is the only pairing that has historically produced better defaults.

Three quantities are worth tracking per task, and the budget arithmetic behind them — including the cascade and routing cost curves — is in the companion note's §8 “Evaluation and cost accounting”:

  • Tokens per solved task — not per request. This is the number that routing and compaction actually change.
  • Position of the load-bearing evidence — if the answer depends on a passage, record where it sat. A U-curve penalty is invisible until someone measures the position.
  • Model tier per call — the fraction of calls that needed the largest model. In most pipelines this is a small number, and it is almost never audited.
The cheap experiment nobody runs: take your production logs and re-run 200 solved tasks through the smallest model that passes your eval. If the pass rate holds, you have found a routing opportunity worth money; if it drops, you have learned which tasks genuinely need capacity. Both outcomes are useful, and the total cost is a few dollars.

5. Specialization by evidence

The case for model specialization, and the orchestrator-versus-single-agent disagreement that follows from it, are made in the companion note's §2 and §6. What is worth separating, when you try to buy that argument, is three different claims with very different amounts of support.

ClaimWhat it meansEvidenceHow to test it locally
Task specializationA small model fine-tuned for one task beats a large general model on that taskSupported for narrow, high-volume, well-labelled tasksHold out 500 labelled items; compare accuracy at equal cost
Role specializationPrompting the same model as a "specialist" improves itWeak; mostly a prompt-formatting effectCompare specialist prompt vs plain instruction at equal tokens
Architectural specializationDifferent agents with different models and contexts beat one agent with all of itConditional — wins when subtasks are separable and contexts would otherwise collideSame task, one agent vs split agents, matched total tokens

The middle row is where most of the industry's language lives and where the least evidence is. Role prompts change formatting and tone reliably, and change reasoning capacity unreliably. The third row is the interesting one, and its condition — separability — is exactly what the orchestrator-versus-single-agent debate is about. Both sides are right about their own workloads.

6. Where 2026 sits

  • Capacity is not the constraint. Windows are large enough for almost any single document task; utilization is the constraint.
  • Routing is the highest-return change for most teams, because it reduces cost without touching the task.
  • Compaction is under-used. Summarize-and-restart beats carrying a long transcript, and it is a ten-line change.
  • Sub-agents win on separable, parallel, breadth-first work and lose on work that needs one coherent context.
  • Role-play specialization is mostly formatting. Task fine-tuning is real; persona prompting is cheap but not a capability multiplier.
  • Put tokens-per-solved-task on the dashboard. It is the only metric that has historically changed defaults.

Closing thoughts

The leaner-agents argument is often read as a bet against capability scaling. It is better read as a bet about where the marginal return is. Once a window is large enough to hold the task, adding tokens to it adds cost and middle, not information; once a model is strong enough to format a record, a bigger model adds latency, not correctness. The measurements that established this were cheap, published, and mostly made on the customer's side of the API.

Which is why the practical advice is so unglamorous: route, compact, and count. The teams that do this are not using weaker models. They are spending the same budget on more attempts, and the attempts are where the accuracy comes from.
