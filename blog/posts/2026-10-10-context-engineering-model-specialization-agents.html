Small Models, Short Contexts: Why Agentic AI Is Getting Leaner

A companion to our survey Swarm Intelligence for AI. That post asked how many agents to wire together; this one asks a sharper, more practical question: which models, and how much context, should each agent actually carry?

The counterintuitive claim gaining traction in 2025–2026 is that agentic systems often work better — not merely cheaper — when you use smaller/faster models for most of the work and keep each agent's context short and clean. A frontier model stays in the driver's seat for planning; fast specialists, sometimes with their own small context windows, do the rest.

This is a thesis with real evidence behind it, and real dissent. Below, the supporting results and the strongest counter-argument are laid out side by side, with peer-reviewed papers, preprints, and engineering write-ups labeled so you can weigh them differently.

1. The big picture

Two independent efficiency pressures are reshaping agent architecture:

  • Model specialization. Not every step in an agent loop needs a frontier model. Routing, cascades, and lead/worker splits assign cheap, fast models to the bulk of the tokens and reserve an expensive model for the hard calls.
  • Context engineering. Long contexts are not free — they cost money, add latency, and degrade reasoning. Decomposing a task so each agent sees a short, relevant window can beat feeding everything into one giant context.

These two pressures reinforce each other. A long context demands a big model to attend to it; a short context lets a small model perform near its best. That is why "small models + short contexts" is emerging as a single design stance rather than two separate tweaks.

2. The case for smaller models

Cascades: match the big model for a fraction of the cost

The foundational result is FrugalGPT (Chen, Zaharia, Zou, arXiv:2305.05176). It frames model choice as an LLM cascade: try a cheap model first, and only escalate to a stronger one when a scorer judges the answer insufficient. The paper reports matching the best individual model's quality (e.g., GPT-4) with up to a 98% cost reduction, or gaining about +4% accuracy at the same cost. Its key observation is that API pricing across models differs by up to two orders of magnitude — so routing decisions dominate the economics of any agentic pipeline.

Routing: choose the cheapest model that suffices

RouteLLM (arXiv:2406.18665) turns that intuition into a trained router that sends easy queries to a weak model and hard ones to a strong model, aiming to preserve quality while cutting cost substantially. In an agent loop, this is the difference between one model doing everything and a planner that dispatches each subtask to a right-sized model.

Small models are improving independently

The premise of "use a small model" rests on small models being good enough. Surveys such as "Small Language Models: Survey, Measurements, and Insights" (arXiv:2409.15790) document how capable the sub-10B class has become on many narrow tasks — which is precisely where high-volume agent steps live.

The design implication: assign models to roles by difficulty, not by uniformity. A frontier model plans and resolves ambiguity; a fast model extracts, formats, retrieves, and executes.

3. The case for shorter contexts

Long context degrades — the middle gets lost

The canonical evidence is "Lost in the Middle" (Liu et al., arXiv:2307.03172, TACL 2023). Performance is highest when the needed information sits at the beginning or end of the context and degrades significantly when it is buried in the middle — and this holds even for models explicitly built for long context. Two consequences follow: stuffing more text into a prompt is not the same as giving the model more usable information, and irrelevant filler actively harms accuracy.

Short contexts per agent: Chain-of-Agents

Chain-of-Agents (Zhang et al., arXiv:2406.02818) makes this operational. A long document is segmented, each chunk is handled by a worker agent that sees only a short context, and a manager agent synthesizes the workers' outputs. The paper reports up to 10% improvements over RAG, full-context, and multi-agent baselines on long-context QA, summarization, and code completion. The mechanism is exactly the efficiency thesis: each agent stays in the regime where it reasons well.

Context management as an explicit engineering discipline

Anthropic's write-up "Effective context engineering for AI agents" generalizes these findings into practice: compaction (summarize and discard), structured external memory, and sub-agent spawning with fresh windows. Related work like MemGPT (arXiv:2310.08560) treats the context window as a managed resource, paging information in and out of external storage.

4. How swarms exploit both at once

The clearest real-world synthesis is Anthropic's engineering report "How we built our multi-agent research system" (published 13 June 2025), which also appears in our swarm survey. Its architecture is a direct illustration of the lean thesis:

  • Model specialization. A lead agent powered by a frontier model (Claude Opus 4) plans and spawns parallel subagents driven by a smaller, faster model (Claude Sonnet 4).
  • Context isolation. Each subagent works in its own context window, so no single agent has to hold the entire task. The lead summarizes completed phases, stores the plan to external memory, and spawns fresh subagents with clean contexts.
  • Filesystem handoff. Subagents write outputs to a shared filesystem rather than passing them through a chain of messages, explicitly to avoid the "game of telephone" that corrupts information.
  • Results. The multi-agent system outperformed a single frontier agent by 90.2% on an internal research evaluation for breadth-first queries. Token usage alone explained 80% of performance variance on BrowseComp; token usage plus tool-call count plus model choice explained 95%.

Two caveats the report itself raises matter enormously. First, the win is compute-driven: agents use about 4× the tokens of chat and multi-agent systems about 15×, so the 90.2% is partly "more thinking," not magic. Second, the approach does not suit domains that need shared context or heavy inter-agent dependencies — most coding among them. That is the seam where the counterpoint begins.

5. Ensembling the cheap

A related line gets gains from many cheap models rather than one expensive one.

  • "More Agents Is All You Need" (Li et al., arXiv:2402.05120, TMLR) shows that a simple sampling-and-voting scheme ("Agent Forest") improves LLM performance simply by instantiating more agents and aggregating their answers. The method is orthogonal to other tricks, and the size of the gain correlates with task difficulty.
  • Mixture-of-Agents (Wang et al., arXiv:2406.04692) stacks layers of LLM agents where each agent reads all previous-layer outputs. Using open-source models only, it reportedly reached 65.1% on AlpacaEval 2.0 versus 57.5% for GPT-4 Omni, and set state of the art on MT-Bench and FLASK.

Both results cut against the "one big model" instinct: aggregation of cheaper outputs can rival or exceed a single frontier pass. Both also reinforce that extra compute is doing real work — the honest comparison is against a single model given the same token budget.

6. The counterpoint: don't build multi-agents

The strongest dissent comes from Walden Yan of Cognition in "Don't Build Multi-Agents" (12 June 2025), an industry essay rather than a peer-reviewed study — but an influential one. Its context-engineering principles deserve to be read alongside the pro-swarm evidence:

  • Share full traces, not fragments. Agents should share complete context and decision history; handing off only individual messages loses the reasoning that justifies the work.
  • Actions carry implicit decisions. When agents act on different assumptions, the conflicting decisions produce bad results. Parallelism multiplies this risk.
  • Prefer a single-threaded, linear agent for many tasks, spending effort on context compression instead of coordination.

Notably, Cognition's answer to the "short context" problem is itself a small model: they fine-tuned a dedicated context-compression model. They also report that a small "edit-apply" model rewriting a whole file from a plain-language description was more reliable than a large model emitting a diff — a concrete case of small models beating large ones on a narrow task. Their critique of OpenAI's Swarm and Microsoft's AutoGen is that dispersed decision-making yields fragile systems.

The reconciliation is not "swarms good" or "swarms bad" but task-dependent: read-heavy, breadth-first, parallelizable work favors swarms of small models with isolated short contexts; write-heavy, dependency-dense work (like code editing) favors one strong model with shared context and aggressive compression. Even Anthropic's own report concedes the coding case.

7. A practical playbook

  • Right-size each role. Frontier model for planning, ambiguity, and final judgment; fast model for retrieval, extraction, formatting, and routine tool calls.
  • Keep each agent's context short and on-topic. Segment long inputs (Chain-of-Agents style) and give each worker only what its subtask needs.
  • Use external memory and compaction. Summarize finished phases, persist plans and artifacts to disk or a store, and spawn fresh agents with clean windows.
  • Prefer structured handoffs over message relay. Write subagent outputs to shared storage to avoid lossy "telephone" chains.
  • Match architecture to dependency structure. Parallel swarms for independent, breadth-first subtasks; single-threaded for tightly coupled work.
  • Budget tokens explicitly. Because token count predicts performance, treat compute as a knob, and compare against a single model at equal spend.

8. Evaluation and cost accounting

The efficiency thesis is easy to overstate, so measurement discipline is essential:

  • Compare at equal cost. A swarm that beats one model may simply have spent 15× the tokens. Report the single-model baseline at the same token budget.
  • Separate model, structure, and context. When a lean system wins, attribute the gain to the model choice, the topology, or the context management — not all three at once.
  • Measure latency, not just quality. A core promise of fast models is responsiveness; parallel tool calling reportedly cut research time by up to 90%, but synchronous coordination and cold starts are real bottlenecks.
  • Watch the middle. If a "long-context" agent underperforms, the culprit may be information buried mid-prompt, not the model.

9. What to watch next

  • Learned routers inside agents. RouteLLM-style controllers becoming a default component of agent frameworks, choosing a model per step.
  • Pressure-tested small models. More head-to-head results like the edit-apply case, mapping exactly where a small model beats a large one.
  • Compression as a first-class model. Dedicated context-compression models (as Cognition proposes) becoming standard infrastructure.
  • Honest multi-agent benchmarks. Evaluations that fix the token budget and separate topology from model, echoing the "Mental-Models for Multi-Agent Systems" evaluation line.
  • Serving-layer gains. Low-latency serving, prefix caching, and batching that make the "same model, faster" part of the thesis measurable independently of model choice.

Closing thoughts

The lean-agent thesis is best stated precisely: right-size the model and shrink the context, and you often get better results per dollar and sometimes better results outright. The evidence runs from cascades and routing that cut cost by an order of magnitude, to long-context degradation that punishes giant prompts, to a production research system that reserves its frontier model for planning and dispatches fast models with their own clean windows.

The dissent is not a refutation but a boundary condition. Diversity of small agents helps independent, breadth-first work; shared context in one strong agent helps tightly coupled work. The winning engineering move is to know which regime you are in.

Three durable lessons:

  • Model choice is a per-step decision, not a per-system one. Route each subtask to the cheapest model that clears the bar.
  • Context is a budget to be spent, not filled. Short, relevant windows beat long, comprehensive ones when reasoning quality is the goal.
  • Always compare at equal cost. Much of the apparent advantage of large swarms is simply more compute — measure it honestly.

If 2026's swarm story was "many agents," its sequel is "the right agents, with the right context, cheaply."
